What has the Church actually said about AI?

Ask this question online and you get vibes. The real answer is a paper trail: three documents in six years, each with a different job, all resting on more than a century of social teaching. Here is the trail with the receipts.

2020: The Rome Call sets principles

The Rome Call for AI Ethics, convened through the Vatican and signed by tech companies, governments, and religious leaders, is a pledge rather than a teaching document. Its six principles gave the conversation a shared vocabulary, starting with the bluntest one:

“1. Transparency. AI systems must be understandable to all.”

The Rome Call for AI Ethics §6 — read the full text

2025: Antiqua et Nova draws the anthropological line

The doctrinal note from the Dicastery for the Doctrine of the Faith and the Dicastery for Culture and Education is the Church's considered answer to a prior question: before asking what AI should do, say what it is, and what it is not.

“Drawing an overly close equivalence between human intelligence and AI risks succumbing to a functionalist perspective, where people are valued based on the work they can perform.”

Antiqua et Nova §34 — read the full text

The note then works through the practical terrain: work, education, healthcare, relationships, warfare. On autonomous weapons it is unusually direct, calling systems that strike targets without human intervention a “cause for grave ethical concern” (§100).

2026: Magnifica Humanitas makes it an encyclical

With his first encyclical, Pope Leo XIV moved AI from a topic the Church comments on to the organizing challenge of a pontificate. The document reads the whole tradition of Catholic social teaching into the AI age, and it does not stop at generalities:

“Sometimes there is talk of ‘artificial moral agents,’ as if machines were able to distinguish between right and wrong… Yet moral judgment cannot be reduced to calculation, for it involves conscience, personal responsibility and the recognition of the other as a person.”

Magnifica Humanitas §198 — read the full text

On AI in war: “the decision to use lethal force cannot be delegated” to opaque or automated systems, and every system used in war must keep its decision process reconstructable so accountability is never collapsed into the machine.

Magnifica Humanitas §200

The encyclical also carries the tradition's clearest statement to date on machine consciousness, covered in its own briefing: Is AI conscious?

The foundation underneath

None of this was improvised for the occasion. The categories doing the work in all three documents, human dignity, the common good, the priority of persons over systems, were codified in the Compendium of the Social Doctrine two decades before ChatGPT:

“The principle of the common good, to which every aspect of social life must be related if it is to attain its fullest meaning, stems from the dignity, unity and equality of all people.”

Compendium of the Social Doctrine §164 — read the full text

Magnifica Humanitas makes the lineage explicit, opening its second chapter with Rerum Novarum, the 1891 encyclical on the industrial revolution's version of the same question: what happens to the worker when the machine arrives (§30).

Where to go next

If you read one document, read Antiqua et Nova; it is the most compact and the most philosophical. If you read two, add Magnifica Humanitas, chapters 3 and 4. For how this teaching meets industry practice, the Rome Call site tracks its signatories and events.
