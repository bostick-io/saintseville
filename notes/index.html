Notes on what enters the corpus

Short commentary from Paul Bostick on the serious thinking that makes it into Saint Seville: what a new document or essay actually claims, why it earned its place here, and what it changes.

August 2026 · Paul Bostick

Where engineering ends

The sharpest thing I read this summer was Joshua Hochschild's “No, AI Isn't Conscious. But Saying So Invites Further Discernment” in the National Catholic Register (June 10, 2026), and the sharpest thing in it is two sentences about Alan Turing. Turing proposed his famous test as a functional rule: treat the machine as if it thinks. He made no claim about whether it actually does. That approach was solid engineering strategy, and it built the industry I work in. It also left a blind spot just past the edge of the engineering, and that blind spot is what SaintSeville.org explores.

Pope Leo says AI is not conscious and, as Hochschild notices, gives no argument for it. I think Hochschild reads the omission correctly. It works as an invitation. The question got parked by the Turing test in 1950 and now it is unparked, sitting at the exact point where the hand off happens, from engineering to thought.

That hand off point is where SaintSeville.org lives. Engineering can tell you what a model does. It cannot tell you what it means that we built one, or what we owe the people on the other side of it. The Catholic tradition has spent two thousand years working out what a human being is, which turns out to be the exact prerequisite for saying what a machine is not. SaintSeville.org catalogues that thinking as it happens, document by document, with citations you can check yourself. That last part matters most. In an age of confident machine text, the receipts are the product.
